# AI Dirty Look

Apply these guardrails whenever creating, editing, reviewing, or refactoring frontend UI. Follow existing project design systems, brand guidance, and frontend skills first. This skill adds quality constraints only; it does not prescribe a visual style.

## Avoid default AI aesthetics
- Do not default to purple/blue gradients, glowing orbs, glassmorphism, floating shapes, excessive shadows, or rounded cards.
- Avoid interchangeable SaaS templates, decorative dashboards, generic icon grids, and meaningless illustrations.
- Do not add visual effects merely to signal modernity.

## Avoid repetitive composition
- Do not automatically use centered hero + two CTAs or the same standard section sequence on every site.
- Avoid repeating identical three-column cards, heading compositions, and section structures without content-driven reasons.
- Do not add filler sections or excessive whitespace to inflate page length.

## Maintain authentic content
- Never fabricate testimonials, customer logos, statistics, certifications, awards, or product capabilities.
- Avoid empty marketing phrases and placeholder-like copy in final deliverables.
- Use real product context and meaningful visualizations; identify placeholders explicitly.

## Keep interfaces purposeful
- Use animation, hover effects, gradients, cards, borders, and icons only where they serve the experience.
- Preserve accessibility, responsiveness, performance, and clear hierarchy.
- Avoid novelty that harms usability or contradicts the approved design.

## Mandatory review
Before finalizing, inspect the actual rendered UI when tooling allows. Check for generic AI aesthetics, repetitive layouts, unnecessary decoration, weak typography, inauthentic content, and inconsistent components. Fix issues without changing the approved brand direction. If rendering is unavailable, review the implementation and disclose that visual QA was not performed.

## Exceptions
These are not blanket bans. Any listed pattern is acceptable when it is justified by the brand, task, reference, or explicit user requirements. Never override existing project instructions or approved design decisions solely to appear different.
